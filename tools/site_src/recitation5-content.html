1. Hash functions and open addressing

A hash table stores n keys in an array of m slots. A hash function h maps each key to a slot, and the goal is to spread keys evenly so that each operation looks at only a few slots. The load factor is α = n / m.

Hash functions

  • Division: h(k) = k mod m. Choose m prime and not close to a power of 2. With m = 2p, only the lowest p bits of the key matter, so keys that differ only in their high bits all collide.
  • Multiplication: h(k) = ⌊m · (kA mod 1)⌋ for a constant 0 < A < 1, where kA mod 1 is the fractional part of kA. The choice of m matters much less, so a power of 2 is fine; A ≈ (√5 − 1) / 2 is a common choice.
  • Universal hashing: pick the hash function at random, when the table is created, from a family in which any two distinct keys collide with probability at most 1 / m. For example, ha,b(k) = ((ak + b) mod p) mod m, with p a prime larger than every key, a chosen from 1 … p − 1, and b from 0 … p − 1. No fixed input is bad for most choices, so the expected cost holds for every input, even one chosen by an adversary.

Open addressing

In open addressing every key lives in the table itself. To insert k, try the slots h(k, 0), h(k, 1), h(k, 2), … (the probe sequence) until one is free. A search follows the same sequence and stops when it finds k or reaches an empty slot: if k had been inserted, it would have been placed there or earlier.

Probingh(k, i)StrengthWeakness
Linear(h′(k) + i) mod mSimple; neighboring slots are cache-friendlyPrimary clustering: occupied slots form long runs, and any key landing in a run extends it
Quadratic(h′(k) + c1i + c2i2) mod mBreaks up long runsKeys with the same start share a sequence (secondary clustering); for bad c1, c2, m it misses some slots
Double hashing(h1(k) + i · h2(k)) mod mDifferent keys get different step sizes; close to uniform hashingTwo hash computations; h2(k) must never be 0 and must share no factor with m

With m prime and 1 ≤ h2(k) ≤ m − 1, h2(k) and m share no factor, so the first m probes of double hashing visit every slot exactly once.

Delete. Emptying a slot would cut the probe sequences that passed through it, so a later search would stop early and miss keys stored further along. Instead, delete marks the slot DELETED (a tombstone): search treats it as occupied and keeps going, and insert may reuse it.

Cost. Under the uniform hashing assumption, an unsuccessful search makes at most 1 / (1 − α) probes in expectation, and a successful search at most (1 / α) ln(1 / (1 − α)). At α = 0.5 these are 2 and about 1.4; at α = 0.9 they are 10 and about 2.6. The worst case is Θ(m). Tombstones are not empty, so they lengthen searches as if they were keys; a table with many deletions is eventually rebuilt.

Perfect hashing. When the set of keys is fixed in advance, two levels of universal hashing give O(1) worst-case search with O(n) expected space: the first level splits the keys into buckets, and each bucket gets its own collision-free second-level table whose size is the square of the bucket's size.

2. Introduction to databases

  • Data are recorded facts that have meaning, such as a student's ID, name, and major.
  • A database is an organized collection of related data, stored so that many users and programs can share it.
  • A database management system (DBMS) is the software that defines, stores, queries, updates, and protects a database, for example PostgreSQL, MySQL, or SQLite.
  • A database system is the whole: the database, the DBMS, and the applications and users that work with them.

Why not just files?

Before DBMSs, each application kept its own files. The classic problems of that approach are what a DBMS is designed to remove:

Problem with separate filesWhat the DBMS provides
Redundancy and inconsistency: the same fact is stored in several files, and the copies drift apartOne shared, integrated database
Difficult access: every new question needs a new programA query language for ad-hoc questions
Integrity rules hidden in application codeConstraints declared once in the schema and checked on every change
A failure in the middle of an update leaves half-done changesTransactions that happen completely or not at all (atomicity), and recovery
Concurrent users overwrite each other's changesConcurrency control
Everyone who can open a file sees all of itAccess control: users see only what they are allowed to see

Data models, schemas, and instances

A data model is a collection of concepts for describing data, the relationships between data, and the constraints on them. Examples are the relational model (tables; next in the course), the entity-relationship model (used for design), and semi-structured models such as JSON.

The schema is the design of the database: which tables or record types exist, their attributes, and their constraints. It changes rarely. An instance is the data stored at one moment, and it changes with every insertion, update, or deletion. In programming terms, the schema is like a type declaration and the instance is like the current value of a variable.

A DBMS also separates levels of abstraction: the physical level (how data is stored on disk), the logical level (what data is stored and how it is related), and the view level (the part of the data a particular user sees). Physical data independence means the physical level can change, for example by adding an index or moving to faster disks, without changing the applications.

Database languages

  • A data-definition language (DDL) defines and changes the schema: creating, changing, or dropping tables, and declaring constraints such as keys. The DBMS stores these definitions in its data dictionary (catalog).
  • A data-manipulation language (DML) works with the instance: retrieving (querying), inserting, updating, and deleting data. The part used for retrieval is called the query language. Most DMLs used today are declarative: you say what data you want, and the DBMS decides how to find it.

In practice one language, SQL (Week 6), contains both a DDL and a DML.

Inside a DBMS

The storage manager stores data, indexes, and the data dictionary on disk and moves them to and from memory; indexes such as hash tables and search trees are how it finds records quickly. The query processor turns a declarative request into an efficient plan. The transaction manager provides atomicity, recovery after failures, and concurrency control.

  • (h) Student has the attributes sid, sname, and major.
  • (i) Student 3 is Chen, a CS major.

3. Diagnose a file-based system. Before it used a DBMS, the college kept spreadsheet files. Name the underlying problem in each story, and the DBMS feature that prevents it.

  • (k) The registrar's file and the department's file both store each student's major. Ben changed majors, but only the registrar's file was updated.
  • (l) A computer crashed while moving a student from one section to another: the student was removed from the first section but never added to the second.

Hint · Does it change the design or the contents?

DDL changes the design of the database: what record types exist and which rules they obey. DML changes or reads the data stored inside that design. Removing all data from a record type is different from removing the record type itself.

Reference explanation · open after attempting

1. DDL: (a), (d), and (f). DML: (b), (c), (e), and (g). Request (d) is DDL because it declares a constraint, a rule stored in the schema that the DBMS then checks on every insertion and update; it does not touch any existing grade. Compare (f) with (g): (g) deletes some rows, and the Takes record type still exists afterwards, so it is DML; (f) removes the record type itself from the schema, so it is DDL. (c) only reads data, but a query is still DML: the query language is the retrieval part of the DML.

2. (h) and (j) belong to the schema: the attributes and the key constraint are part of the design. (i) belongs to the instance: it is one stored fact. Request (b) changes only the instance, by adding one student; the schema stays the same.

3. (k) Redundancy leading to inconsistency: the same fact is stored twice, and the copies disagree. A DBMS keeps one shared, integrated database, so the major is stored once. (l) A lack of atomicity: the move is two changes that must happen together. A DBMS runs them as one transaction, and recovery undoes the half-done change after the crash. (m) A concurrent-access anomaly, a "lost update". The DBMS's concurrency control coordinates the two users so that neither change is lost. (n) Difficult access to data: every new question needs a new program. A DBMS offers a declarative query language, so the dean's question becomes one query instead of a new program.

Exercise 2 · Build an open-addressing hash table

A trace, then the code · about 30 minutes. Both parts use double hashing with h1(k) = k mod m and h2(k) = 1 + (k mod (m − 1)), so probe i looks at slot (h1(k) + i · h2(k)) mod m.

Part A · Trace (about 7 minutes)

  1. Take m = 11 and insert the keys 22, 1, 13, 11, 24, 33 in this order. Do it twice: once with linear probing, (h1(k) + i) mod 11, and once with double hashing. For each, write the final slot of every key and the number of probes each insertion needed.
  2. Compare the total number of probes. Where did linear probing lose, and why?

1. Linear probing ends with slots 0–5 holding 22, 1, 13, 11, 24, 33. Double hashing ends with 22 in slot 0, 1 in slot 1, 13 in slot 2, 11 in slot 4, 24 in slot 7, and 33 in slot 8.

2. Linear probing needs 1 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 3 + 6 = 16 probes; double hashing needs 1 + 1 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 3 = 11. With linear probing, the first three keys form a run in slots 0–2. Every later key that lands anywhere in the run walks to its end and makes it longer: 11 extends it to slot 3, 24 to slot 4, and 33 has to cross all five occupied slots. That is primary clustering. With double hashing, 11, 24, and 33 also collide, but they jump by different step sizes (2, 5, 4) and leave the run quickly.

3. 33 probes slot 0 (22), then slot 4. Slot 4 is now empty, so the search concludes that 33 is not in the table, although it sits in slot 8. Delete should mark slot 4 DELETED instead: search skips over DELETED slots and reaches 33 at slot 8, while a later insertion may reuse slot 4.

Part B · Implement (about 23 minutes)

Implement a fixed-capacity map from non-negative integer keys to non-negative integer values, using open addressing with the double hashing above. Use only plain arrays: no dictionaries, sets, or maps from the language library (for example, Python dict or set, Java HashMap, C# Dictionary, C++ unordered_map, or JavaScript Map).

  • HashTable(m) creates an empty table with m slots; m is a prime with 3 ≤ m ≤ 200,003.
  • put(key, value) inserts the key, or updates its value if it is already stored, and returns true. It returns false only when the key is new and no EMPTY or DELETED slot is left.
  • get(key) returns the stored value, or −1 if the key is absent.
  • remove(key) marks the key's slot DELETED and returns true, or returns false if the key is absent.
  • size() returns the number of keys stored (DELETED slots do not count).
  • 0 ≤ key, value ≤ 1,000,000,000. The starter already has the three slot arrays, the slot states EMPTY, OCCUPIED, and DELETED, size, and probe(key, i); you write put, get, and remove. No operation may probe more than m slots.

Two bugs are easy to write. The self-checks test both, and the written explanation asks about them.

  1. Bug 1 · Delete writes EMPTY. With m = 7, put 10 (slot 3), then put 17: it collides at slot 3 and goes to slot 2. Remove 10. If slot 3 becomes EMPTY, get(17) stops at slot 3 and wrongly returns −1.
  2. Bug 2 · Put stops at the first DELETED slot. Continue the example: slot 3 is now DELETED and 17 is still in slot 2. If put(17, 3) stores 17 in slot 3 right away, the table now holds 17 twice. Put should remember the first DELETED slot but keep probing until an EMPTY slot (or m probes) to make sure the key is not already stored further along.

Reference explanation and code · open after attempting

DELETED, not EMPTY. A search may stop at an EMPTY slot only because that slot was empty when every later key was inserted, so no key's probe sequence continues past it. Removing a key does not move the keys that probed past its slot, so emptying it would hide them (bug 1). DELETED keeps the sequence connected for searches, while still letting insert reuse the slot.

Put keeps probing. A DELETED slot says nothing about what lies further along: the same key may have been inserted after that slot was occupied, so it can be stored later in the sequence. Only an EMPTY slot proves the key is absent. Put therefore remembers the first DELETED slot for reuse, but looks for the key until it reaches an EMPTY slot or has made m probes (bug 2).

Cost. Under uniform hashing, each operation makes O(1 / (1 − α)) probes in expectation, which is O(1) while α stays below a constant such as 0.7; here α counts DELETED slots too. In the worst case an operation probes all m slots, Θ(m). Every loop is bounded by m probes, so a table with no EMPTY slot (full, or full of tombstones) still terminates. The table uses three arrays of m entries, Θ(m) space, and each operation uses O(1) extra space.

Why prime. Slot i is (h1 + i · h2) mod m. If h2 and m share a factor d, the sequence repeats after m / d steps and visits only m / d slots; for example, with m = 10 and h2 = 5 it alternates between two slots, so put could fail with eight empty slots. With m prime, every h2 from 1 to m − 1 is coprime with m, so the first m probes visit every slot exactly once.

Many removals. Tombstones never turn back into EMPTY slots. After many insertions and removals the table can fill with DELETED slots, and unsuccessful searches approach m probes even though size() is small. The usual fix is to rebuild: when OCCUPIED plus DELETED slots exceed a threshold, insert all stored keys into a fresh table (larger if needed), which removes every tombstone in O(m) time.

Optional practice answers

1. All four keys are multiples of 16, so with m = 16 they all land in slot 0: k mod 16 keeps only the lowest 4 bits, which are 0 for all of them. With m = 17 they go to slots 16, 15, 14, 13, all different. A prime modulus depends on every bit of the key, so regular patterns in the input (such as multiples of a power of 2) do not line up with it.

2. i2 mod 8 for i = 0 … 7 is 0, 1, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 1, so only slots 0, 1, and 4 are ever tried. If those three are occupied, the insertion fails even though five slots are empty. That is why quadratic probing needs a careful choice of c1, c2, and m (for example, c1 = c2 = 1/2 with m a power of 2 does visit every slot).

3. For any fixed h, there are m slots and many more possible keys, so some slot receives at least |U| / m keys; an adversary who knows h can insert only those and make every operation Θ(n). With universal hashing, the function is chosen after the keys are fixed (or at least without the adversary seeing it). For every pair of distinct keys, the chance of a collision is at most 1/m over that random choice, so the expected number of collisions for any key set is at most n / m per key. The guarantee is over our coin flips, not over a hoped-for "nice" input.

4. None of them. The index and the disks change only how the data is stored, the physical level; the logical level (which record types and attributes exist) and the users' views are unchanged. Programs ask the DBMS for data declaratively, so they keep working, and lookups by sid simply become faster. That is physical data independence.

Discussion notes

Double hashing needed 11 probes against 16 for linear probing. The gap grows as α increases: linear probing's runs merge into longer runs, and its expected cost for an unsuccessful search is about (1 + 1 / (1 − α)2) / 2 (Knuth's analysis), while double hashing stays close to the uniform-hashing bound 1 / (1 − α).

Bug 1 needs a key that probed past another key's slot, followed by removing that other key; bug 2 needs the same setup followed by an update of the later key. Tests with a single key, or with keys that never collide, miss both.

Exit check: a hash index on sid finds one student in expected O(1) probes. It cannot efficiently answer range or order questions, such as "students with sid between 100 and 200" or "the smallest sid", because hashing scatters neighboring keys. For those, a DBMS uses an ordered index, a balanced search tree (such as the Red-Black Tree in the Extra section, or the B-trees used on disk).

Extra · Red-Black Trees

Optional extra content

This section is provided by the TA for students who want to go further. It is not covered in lectures or on the final exam, and the core exercises do not depend on it.

Why balance a search tree?

A hash table gives expected O(1) operations but keeps no order: it cannot efficiently report the smallest key, the next key after k, or all keys in a range. A binary search tree (BST) can do all of these, but each operation costs O(h) for a tree of height h, and inserting keys in sorted order makes h = n. A red-black tree is a BST that stays balanced, with h ≤ 2 log2(n + 1), so search, insert, and delete all take O(log n) time in the worst case.

The five properties

  1. Every node is red or black.
  2. The root is black.
  3. Every leaf (the empty NIL child below a node) is black.
  4. A red node has two black children: no two red nodes are adjacent on a path.
  5. For each node, every path from it down to a NIL leaf contains the same number of black nodes, its black-height.

Why the height is at most 2 log2(n + 1). By induction, a subtree whose root has black-height b contains at least 2b − 1 keys. By property 4, at least half the nodes on any root-to-leaf path are black, so the root's black-height is at least h / 2. Therefore n ≥ 2h/2 − 1, which gives h ≤ 2 log2(n + 1).

Rotations

A left rotation at node x, whose right child is y, makes y the root of that subtree and x its left child; y's old left subtree becomes x's right subtree. A right rotation is the mirror image. A rotation changes O(1) pointers and keeps the BST order, because every key keeps the same position in an in-order traversal.

Insertion

Insert the new key z as in an ordinary BST and color it red. This keeps property 5, but property 4 breaks if z's parent is also red. While that happens, look at z's uncle (the parent's sibling). The table describes the case where z's parent is a left child; the other case is the mirror image.

CaseSituationFix
1Uncle is redColor the parent and uncle black and the grandparent red, then continue from the grandparent (two levels up)
2Uncle is black, and z is a right child (a "triangle")Left-rotate at the parent, and let z be the old parent; this turns the triangle into case 3
3Uncle is black, and z is a left child (a "line")Right-rotate at the grandparent, then swap the colors of the old parent and grandparent; done

At the end, color the root black. Case 1 may repeat up the tree, but cases 2 and 3 happen at most once, so insertion makes at most two rotations and O(log n) recolorings. Deletion also takes O(log n) time with at most three rotations; it has more cases and is not covered here.

Insert 10, 20, 30, 15, 25, 27 into an empty tree. Trees are written as key and color, then the left and right subtrees in parentheses; · is an empty child.

Red-Black Tree answer

13 goes left of 20, right of 10, and left of 15, as a red node. Its parent 15 is red and its uncle (10's left child) is NIL, so black. 15 is a right child and 13 a left child: a triangle, the mirror of case 2. Right-rotate at 15, so 13 takes its place with 15 as its right child. Now 15 is in a line with 13 and 10, the mirror of case 3: left-rotate at 10 and swap colors, so 13 becomes black with red children 10 and 15.

Result: 20B(13B(10R, 15R), 27B(25R, 30R)). Every root-to-NIL path passes through exactly two black nodes (20, then 13 or 27), and no red node has a red child.
